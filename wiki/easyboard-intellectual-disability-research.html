Travel independence for people with intellectual disabilities

What we learned from the research, what EasyBoard should build next, and how to test it with the people it is for.

01 / Summary

The short version

EasyBoard already has the right pieces: photo places, a simple route choice, one-tap calling, and location sharing. The next step is to turn those pieces into a calm travel coach. The app should decide what to show next.

Promise 01
“I always know the next step.”

One instruction at a time, with a photo of the place, a spoken version, and a clear button for what to do now.

Promise 02
“I can get help without explaining everything.”

A help screen that shows the person’s name, destination, location and caregiver’s number. One tap to call. One tap to show a stranger.

Promise 03
“I decide what my caregiver can see.”

Sharing starts with a choice. The person can see what is shared, pause it, and know who is watching.

Build first

Five changes that matter most

  1. Guided Trip Mode. One step per screen, with a boarding confirmation, photo, audio, progress, and an “I’m not sure” button.
  2. Read Aloud and Easy Read. Short sentences, large text, optional images, and speech on every important screen.
  3. Help Card. A full-screen card the person can show to a driver, staff member, or stranger.
  4. Trip Story. A visual preview of the whole trip before it starts, with photos from the caregiver.
  5. Calm change alerts. Plain words when a bus is late or a route changes, plus one clear action.
Avoid

What not to build

  • Always-on location by default. It turns support into surveillance and can push people to stop using the app.
  • Dense route cards. “Route 1, estimated distance, estimated journey time” is a planning screen, not a travelling screen.
  • Red alarm states for normal problems. A late bus is not an emergency. Calm words and a next action work better.
  • Text-only instructions. Reading is not the only way to understand. Pair text with photos and audio.
  • A caregiver-only product. The person with a disability is the user. The caregiver is a supporter.

02 / Users

Who we design for

People with intellectual disabilities are not one group. Reading levels, phone skills, anxiety, support needs, and family rules vary. Design for the edges and the middle gets easier. These personas are composites built from the research, not real customers.

R

Ryan, 16

Student · mild ID
What he needs
To get from school to home on bus 2 without a teacher following him.
What gets in the way
He recognises some numbers but not all. He gets anxious when the bus does not come. He waits until the last second to press the bell.
Design clue
Show a photo of the stop and the bus number. Tell him how many stops are left. Give him a practice run before the solo run.
S

Siti, 29

Works in a café · mild ID
What she needs
To meet friends after work without her mother watching every move.
What gets in the way
She reads short sentences. She does not want to be tracked all day. She has been spoken over by staff who assumed she could not decide.
Design clue
Let her choose what is shared and for how long. Use plain words. Make “pause sharing” as easy as “start sharing”.
M

Mei Leng, 58

Transition coordinator
What she needs
To set up a route once, teach it, and know when a student is stuck.
What gets in the way
She cannot watch a live dot for 30 students. She needs exceptions, not a control room.
Design clue
Alerts for “not moving for 10 minutes”, “off route”, “arrived”, and “asked for help”. A trip card, not a map wall.
T

Mr Tan, 62

Parent and caregiver
What he needs
To know his son is safe without calling him every 10 minutes.
What gets in the way
He worries about traffic, strangers, and the phone battery dying. He does not want to be a spy.
Design clue
Simple status: “On the bus”, “10 minutes away”, “Arrived”. A voice note is faster and warmer than a text.

Design for different support needs. Some people will use the app alone. Some will use it with a support worker sitting beside them. Some will not read at all. The same journey should work with touch, voice, pictures, and a phone call.

03 / Findings

What the research says

Ten findings, each with the evidence behind it and what it changes in the product. The pattern is consistent: the barriers are less about maps and more about uncertainty, memory, communication, control, and the small transitions that apps skip, like boarding.

01

Travel is a chain of small transitions, not one route

A scoping review of 34 studies found barriers at every link of the travel chain: walking to the stop, waiting, boarding, riding, getting off, and walking to the destination. The barriers included long waits, missing information, unclear signage, crowding, and driver attitudes.[2] The transport-tech review makes the same point: most studies test one context, such as a single bus route or pedestrian travel, not a full multi-modal trip.[3]

For EasyBoardThe unit of design is the transition, not the route line. “Walk to the stop. Wait here. Get on bus 2. Get off in 8 stops.” Each one needs its own screen and its own help.
02

The hardest part is uncertainty, not distance

In a UK Department for Transport study, people with learning disabilities and other non-visible impairments said the lack of real-time information, route changes, and delays caused the most stress. Some avoided buses completely. Journey apps helped because they reduced the amount of information people had to hold in their heads.[7]

For EasyBoardWhen something changes, do not show a new route. Show one sentence: “The bus is 6 minutes late. Wait here.” Then one action: “Tell me when it comes.”
03

Asking for help is the hardest step

The same UK study found that communicating with staff or strangers caused anxiety and embarrassment. Participants avoided asking questions and used apps or called family instead. They suggested free assistance cards that tell staff what the person needs without a long explanation.[7] The JAM Card scheme in Ireland now has more than 35,000 cards and app registrations; the card lets a person ask for “just a minute” of patience.[10]

For EasyBoardA help screen is a social tool. It should be readable at arm’s length, work without speech, and say exactly what the person needs.
04

Route knowledge is fragile, and practice builds it

At MINDS, a student needed 16 weeks of travel training to go from school to home alone. He could not recognise all bus numbers and wanted to board every bus with a “2”. The team used placards, repeated the route twice a week, and simulated problems such as missing a stop.[8] The research review found that travel training improved confidence and use of public transport.[2]

For EasyBoardThe app should support rehearsal as well as live navigation. A practice mode with the caregiver, a “what if” quiz, and a “solo ready” checklist fits how travel skills are actually learned.
05

Context-aware prompts work, but the evidence is small

A review of 13 studies found that digital tools improved independent travel. In one small study, 8 of 11 participants using a GPS device completed the bus route; in the control group, 1 of 12 got off at the correct stop. In another, 17 of 18 participants using an augmented-reality app reached and identified the destination, compared with 9 of 18 using Google Maps. A third found 93.3% wayfinding success with a PDA and QR tags; four of six participants failed without the aid.[3] These are promising but small studies, often on one route.

For EasyBoardUse GPS to trigger prompts, but do not depend on it. Let the person tap “I’m here” when GPS drifts. Always keep a caregiver call and a human fallback.
06

Most travel apps are not designed with this group

In the 13-study review, only 2 studies involved people with intellectual disabilities in the design of the technology. Most focused on a single context and did not explore communication, buying tickets, crowds, or asking for help.[3] A separate study of ride-hailing apps found that people with intellectual disabilities hit their own difficulties, and recommended family and government support.[4]

For EasyBoardCo-design is part of the work. Test with people with different reading levels and support needs. Pay them for their time. Ship the version they can use on a real route.
07

Easy Read is a system, not a font size

NHS England guidance says easy read should use short sentences of about 15 words, one idea per sentence, simple punctuation, active language, and images that match the text. It should be tested with the audience. Images can distract some autistic people, so easy read should be a choice, not a forced mode.[6] The CDC guide for adults with intellectual disabilities and very low literacy is stricter: aim for 10 words or fewer per sentence, one idea per screen, literal images, and one action per screen.[18] The evidence that easy read improves understanding is limited. A 2026 systematic review of 29 studies found that people valued easy read, but the research quality was low and the effect was unclear.[21] The W3C cognitive accessibility guidance adds consistent layouts, clear headings, familiar controls, and help that is easy to find.[5]

For EasyBoardBuild an Easy Read setting that changes text, spacing, and images across the app. Keep an audio version. Test the actual screens with people with different reading levels; do not assume pictures help everyone.
08

Safety tools can become control tools

Research on surveillance in care settings finds a persistent tension between safety and freedom. Professionals worry about restriction, and the ideal use depends on whether everyone affected understands what the technology is for.[12] Adults with intellectual disabilities describe carer gatekeeping and restriction as harmful to their wellbeing. They want support to manage risk, not removal of the chance to take it.[11]

For EasyBoardMake sharing consensual, visible, and time-limited. Default to “share this trip”, not “share my location forever”. Let the person pause without a fight.
09

Digital exclusion is part of the design brief

People with intellectual disabilities remain more digitally excluded than the general population, and support staff sometimes restrict access because of perceived risk.[11][13] In an Icelandic survey of 144 people with intellectual disabilities, 25.2% said they never or almost never used phone or digital technology to contact others.[20] A phone can run out of battery, lose signal, or run out of data. Not everyone has the latest device.

For EasyBoardCache the route. Make the help card work offline. Provide an SMS fallback and a printable card. The safety plan cannot depend on a live connection.
10

Boarding is its own step

Travel-training assessments list “knows the bus number”, “identifies the bus and indicates a desire to board”, and “taps the pass” as separate skills.[22] The same manual teaches a specific signal (hold the pass in front of your chest) and a specific cue (wait until the vehicle stops before approaching).[22] A 15-step bus task analysis treats every step as essential; missing one step stops the trip.[26] Boarding is a distinct phase in travel instruction.[23] Pictures at retrieval improve prospective memory for people with intellectual disabilities, so the prompt should appear as a photo at the moment the door opens.[24] TransLink built a virtual bus tour for people who are not comfortable boarding, which shows the demand for rehearsal.[25]

For EasyBoardMake boarding its own step. Wait for the bus to stop. Show the open door. Say “Get on bus 2.” Ask “Are you on the bus?” If there is no confirmation in 45 seconds, prompt again and tell the caregiver. Never treat “I see the bus” as the end of the step.

Patterns to copy

  • Personal photos and recorded voice, not generic icons.
  • One step at a time, with the current and next step visible.
  • Let people rehearse boarding: the door, the card tap, and the seat.
  • Manual advance when GPS is uncertain or steps are close.
  • An explicit, user-initiated route to a human.
  • Trip sharing that the person can see and stop.

Patterns to avoid

  • Turn arrows as the only cue. They are abstract and easy to misread.
  • Automatic step changes based only on GPS. Close steps cause confusion.
  • Camera AR as the only way to navigate. Device, country, and coverage limits exclude people.
  • Always-on sharing as the default. It turns support into surveillance.

One more local finding. Singapore already has schemes that do part of this job: the Helping Hand identifiers (including “Please alert me when I am approaching my stop”), MINDS travel training, the Travel Makers volunteering programme, and Heart Zones. The SBS Transit Travel Buddy is currently focused on wheelchair users; it may extend to other disabilities. EasyBoard should link to these schemes and copy their language, not invent a parallel system.[1][9][8][17]

04 / Principles

Eight rules for the product

These rules turn the research into decisions. When a design debate gets stuck, pick the option that follows the rule. The rules are written for the whole team.

One screen, one decision

Every screen asks for one thing. If a screen has two primary buttons, it has two screens inside it. Split it.

Show the next step, not the whole map

A map is a planning tool. A step is a travelling tool. Show the map when the person asks “where am I?”, not by default.

Calm words, calm colours

Red is for danger. A late bus is not danger. Use plain sentences, one action, and a colour that does not shout.

Help is a normal action

Put “I need help” on the main screen. Do not hide it behind a menu or a long press. Asking for help is part of travelling, not a failure.

The person is the user

The caregiver sets things up and gets alerts. The person with a disability chooses where to go, starts the trip, and decides what to share.

Privacy is visible

Show a clear “Sharing with Mei Leng” badge while sharing is on. Make pause as easy as start. Explain what is shared in one sentence.

Works when the network doesn’t

Cache the route, the help card, and the caregiver number. Offer SMS and a phone call. A safety feature has to work on a weak connection.

Test with people with intellectual disabilities, and pay them

Include people who read little, people who use AAC (communication aids), and people who travel alone. Run the test on a real route.

A useful test for any new screen. Cover the screen and ask the person: “What can you do here?” If they cannot answer in one sentence, the screen is not finished.

05 / Today

What EasyBoard does now

An audit of the current app, based on the code in runtimes/app. The foundation is good. The gaps are mostly about how information is presented, not about missing maps.

What already works

  • Photo cards for saved places. A picture of home, work, or school is easier than an address.
  • A short home screen. The person picks a place and goes. That is the right shape.
  • One-tap caregiver call. No searching for a number under pressure.
  • Auto-stop location sharing. Sharing ends when the person is within 20 metres of the destination.
  • Caregiver dashboard. A caregiver can see who is online, call, and locate.
  • System text scaling is on by default. React Native text grows with the phone setting, and text-only buttons have a 48 px minimum height. The risk is fixed-height layout, not the setting.
  • One codebase. iOS, Android, and web share the same screens, so fixes reach everyone.

What the audit found

  • No accessibility labels. A screen reader has little to announce beyond the raw text.
  • All-caps buttons. “START NAVIGATION” and “CALL CAREGIVER” are harder to read than sentence case.
  • Route cards are for planners. “Route 1”, “Estimated distance”, and “Estimated journey time” do not help someone standing at a bus stop.
  • Steps are text-only. The directions screen asks the person to read and tap “Next Step”. There is no boarding confirmation, so seeing the bus can look like the end of the step.
  • Help means one phone call. There is no card to show a stranger and no SMS fallback.
  • Sharing has no consent screen. The toggle exists, but the person is not told who sees what, for how long.
  • Sharing may not stop cleanly. The background location task is not stopped on every cleanup path, and the arrival auto-stop is not wired to the route destination. The notification text still contains “xxx”.
  • Large text can clip. Text scaling is on by default, but fixed heights and one-line truncation break the layout at large sizes.

06 / Features

What to build

Nine features, in three priority groups. P0 is the next release. P1 follows when P0 is stable with real users. Each feature names the problem, the behaviour, the risk, and the signal that it worked.

P0

Guided Trip Mode

The current directions screen is a list of text steps. It assumes the person can read, remember where they are in the list, and decide when to move on. That is the exact set of skills the research says is hard.

What it does. Turns a route into a sequence of single-purpose cards. Each card has one instruction, a photo of the place, a spoken version, and one clear action. The app advances when the phone reaches a place (a geofence), on a tap, or after a caregiver confirms. Bus arrival is a tap or a live vehicle feed, never a geofence alone. Boarding is its own step. The app asks for a boarding confirmation.

  1. Before the trip: show the Trip Story and a short readiness check (fare card, phone battery, headphones).
  2. At each step: one sentence, one photo, one audio button, and “I did it”.
  3. When the bus arrives: wait for it to stop. Show the photo of the open door. Say “Get on bus 2.” Ask “Are you on the bus?” A confirmation moves the trip forward. No confirmation after 45 seconds triggers a gentle prompt and a caregiver alert.
  4. If the person is unsure: “I’m not sure” opens three options: show where I am, call my caregiver, or show my help card.
  5. If a step is missed: do not recalculate the whole route. Say “Stay on the bus. We will call you.” and alert the caregiver.
  6. At arrival: “You are here” with the destination photo, and a caregiver notification.

“Walk to the bus stop. It is 3 minutes away.” · “Wait for bus 2. It will come in 4 minutes.” · “The bus has stopped. The door is open. Get on bus 2.” · “Hold your card in front of your chest so the driver sees you.” · “Get off in 8 stops. I will count with you.”

Effort
Large. New trip model, step player, geofences, offline cache.
Evidence
Context-aware prompts and step-by-step guidance improved independent travel in small studies.[3] Travel-training assessments treat “identifies the bus and indicates a desire to board” as a separate skill, and photos at retrieval support prospective memory.[22][24]
Risk
GPS drift triggers the wrong step. The person may also tap “I am on the bus” before boarding. Mitigation: manual advance, a 45-second boarding check, a photo of the open door, and a caregiver voice note.
Success signal
More trips completed without a caregiver call. More bus arrivals followed by boarding within 60 seconds. Shorter recovery time after a missed stop.
P0

Easy Read and Read Aloud

Easy read changes how a screen reads. It needs short sentences, one idea per screen, and images that match the words. The NHS guidance asks for about 15 words per sentence and testing with the audience.[6] The CDC guide is stricter for people with very low literacy: 10 words or fewer, one idea per screen, and literal images.[18] The evidence is mixed, so test it with the people who will use it.[21]

What it does. A global setting that changes copy, spacing, and images across the app. Every important screen gets a speaker button. The setting is per person, not per device, so it follows the account.

  1. Text: sentence case, active voice, one action per sentence, no jargon or acronyms.
  2. Layout: larger type, more line spacing, shorter line length, one column.
  3. Images: optional. Some people find pictures helpful; some autistic people find them distracting. Let the person choose.
  4. Audio: native text-to-speech for every instruction, error, and button label. Respect the phone’s language and speaking rate.
  5. Fallback: if speech is unavailable, show the text and offer a caregiver voice note.
Effort
Medium. Copy deck, TTS service, design tokens, QA on both platforms.
Evidence
NHS England easy read guidance; W3C COGA design patterns for clear purpose, familiar controls, and help.[6][5]
Risk
Text-to-speech mispronounces names and places. Mitigation: caregiver can record a name; show the written name too.
Success signal
Task success on the top five journeys. Fewer “what does this mean?” questions in testing.
P0

Help Card

People avoid asking strangers or staff for help because it is embarrassing and hard to explain. A card does the explaining for them. The UK research recommended free assistance cards; the JAM Card shows the model works at scale.[7][10]

What it does. A full-screen card with the person’s first name, photo (optional), destination, current area, caregiver name and number, and a plain sentence: “I need help getting to Work. Please call Mei Leng.” The person can show it, read it, or have it read aloud. A second card is for the driver: “I am going to Work. Please tell me when to get off.”

  1. Open: one tap from the home screen and from every step.
  2. Show: large text, high contrast, screen stays awake for 60 seconds.
  3. Call: a single button to the caregiver. If there is no answer, offer the second contact.
  4. Send: SMS with the card text and a map link; works when data is weak.
  5. Emergency: a separate, clearly labelled button for 999, not mixed with the caregiver call.
Effort
Small to medium. Mostly UI, SMS deep link, offline cache.
Evidence
UK DfT assistance-card recommendation; JAM Card adoption; local Helping Hand identifiers.[7][10][1]
Risk
Oversharing personal details to strangers. Mitigation: first name only, no home address, caregiver can turn off the photo.
Success signal
Help card opened on real trips. Successful handovers to staff or caregivers. People say they would use it again.
P0

Trip Story

A route line is an abstract drawing. A story is a sequence of places the person can recognise. The MINDS travel training example shows the power of photos, placards, and repetition.[8]

What it does. Before the trip, shows a visual timeline: walk to the stop, wait for bus 2, get on bus 2, tap the card, sit near the driver, ride 8 stops, get off near the blue library, walk 2 minutes, arrive. Each item has a photo and an optional caregiver voice note.

  1. Create: the caregiver adds or confirms up to 8 steps and a photo for each.
  2. Preview: the person can swipe through the story before starting.
  3. Practice: “Practice this trip” runs the same story with quiz questions, including “the bus stops, what do you do?”
  4. Start: the story becomes the Guided Trip Mode. The first step is already open.
Boarding is a step. The story shows the open door, the card tap, and the seat. The live trip asks for confirmation. A generic map pin is not a landmark.
Effort
Medium. Media upload, step editor, offline storage.
Evidence
Photo and landmark prompts improved navigation in small studies; travel training improved confidence.[3][2] A meta-analysis of visual activity schedules found a large effect on independent skills (Tau-U 0.96).[19] Virtual bus tours are used to rehearse boarding, and pictures at retrieval support prospective memory.[25][24]
Risk
Old photos confuse the person. Mitigation: “photo updated” date; caregiver review before each term.
Success signal
More people start a trip after viewing the story. Fewer wrong-stop incidents. Fewer boarding delays.
P0

Calm change alerts

Uncertainty is the main source of anxiety. A late bus, a platform change, or a missed stop can end the trip. The UK research found that real-time information and clear alternatives reduced stress.[7]

What it does. Watches the trip for delays, route changes, and deviations. When something changes, it shows one calm sentence and one action. It does not redraw the map or show a red warning.

  1. Delay: “The bus is 6 minutes late. Wait here. I will tell you when it comes.”
  2. Missed stop: “You passed your stop. Stay on the bus. We will call you.” The caregiver gets the same message.
  3. Off route: “You are near the MRT station. Are you okay?” with “Yes” and “I need help”.
  4. No movement: after 10 minutes, ask the person first. Only alert the caregiver if there is no answer or the person asks.
Effort
Medium. Location rules, message templates, escalation logic.
Evidence
UK DfT real-time information findings; W3C COGA error prevention and clear next steps.[7][5]
Risk
False alarms annoy caregivers and frighten the person. Mitigation: confidence thresholds, “I’m okay” button, caregiver confirms before calling.
Success signal
Fewer panic calls. Faster recovery. Caregivers say alerts are useful, not noisy.
P1

Caregiver Co-pilot

A live dot does not tell a caregiver what is happening. Caregivers need to know “is he on the bus?”, “is he stuck?”, “when will he arrive?”. Watching a dot all day does not scale to a classroom or a family with two jobs.[1]

What it does. Replaces the dashboard with trip cards. Each card shows the current stage, ETA, last update, and one action. The map is one tap away.

  1. Trip card: “Ryan is on bus 2. 12 minutes to Work.”
  2. Alerts: started, arrived, asked for help, off route, no movement 10 minutes, bus arrived but not boarded after 45 seconds.
  3. Voice note: “Press the green button when you see the blue gate.”
  4. “I’m worried” button: escalates to a call, not a silent alert.
Effort
Medium. Depends on the trip model from Guided Trip Mode.
Evidence
Caregiver burden and the need for exceptions, not continuous monitoring.[1][12]
Risk
Becomes surveillance by another name. Mitigation: trip-only sharing, visible consent, no history by default.
Success signal
Fewer “where are you?” calls. Caregivers respond to alerts within 2 minutes.
P1

Practice Mode

Travel training works through repetition, modelling, and safe mistakes. MINDS used 16 weeks of accompanied trips and simulated problems such as missing a stop. An app can support that routine between sessions.[8]

What it does. A rehearsal flow for a saved route. The person and caregiver walk through the story, answer “what do you do next?” questions, and mark the trip as “ready to try alone”.

  1. Rehearse: the story plays with photos and audio, without live location.
  2. Quiz: “The bus does not come. What do you do?” with three picture answers.
  3. Simulate: “You missed your stop. Stay on the bus. Call Mei Leng.”
  4. Sign-off: the caregiver marks the route as practised. The home screen labels it “The way we practised”.
Effort
Medium. Reuses Trip Story and quiz content.
Evidence
Travel training improved self-efficacy; video and photo prompts improved route learning.[2][3]
Risk
Quiz success is mistaken for real-world readiness. Mitigation: caregiver sign-off and a real accompanied trip.
Success signal
More first solo trips after practice. Fewer help requests on practised routes.
P1

Voice notes and landmark photos

A caregiver’s voice is familiar and can carry meaning that text cannot. “Turn left at the blue gate” is easier than “continue for 120 metres”. Personal landmarks also solve the last-100-metres problem that maps handle badly.

What it does. Lets the caregiver attach a short voice note and a photo to any step. The person hears the note at the right moment, with a replay button.

  1. Record: up to 15 seconds per step, with a transcript.
  2. Attach: photo, voice note, and a short label.
  3. Play: automatically at the step, or on tap. Never autoplay in public without headphones.
  4. Update: caregiver can replace a note when the route changes.
Effort
Medium. Audio recording, storage, playback, transcript.
Evidence
Visual and auditory prompts are the most common design elements in the transport-tech review.[3]
Risk
Audio privacy in public; unclear speech; language mismatch. Mitigation: headphones prompt, transcript, re-record.
Success signal
Voice notes played on real steps. Fewer wrong turns near the destination.
P1

Privacy Center

Location sharing is useful only if the person trusts it. Research on surveillance in care finds a tension between safety and autonomy, and adults with intellectual disabilities describe restriction as harmful.[12][11]

What it does. One screen that answers three questions: What does my caregiver see? When? Who else can see it? It uses three presets and one pause button. For people who cannot consent, the caregiver and a trusted person record the decision and who agreed.

  1. Trip only: share while a trip is active. Stop at arrival. This is the default.
  2. Location always: share until I turn it off. Clear badge on the home screen.
  3. History: off by default. The caregiver sees the current trip, not everywhere the person has been.
  4. Pause: one tap. The caregiver sees “Sharing paused by Ryan”, not a blank map.
Effort
Small to medium. Mostly UI and data rules.
Evidence
Surveillance ethics; supported decision-making; visible consent.[12][11][16]
Risk
Too many choices. Mitigation: three presets, one sentence each, caregiver cannot change without the person seeing it.
Success signal
People can explain what is shared. Pause is used without conflict. Fewer people turn sharing off entirely.

Sequencing matters. Guided Trip Mode and the Help Card need the trip model and offline cache. Easy Read and Read Aloud touch every screen, so do them early and reuse the copy deck. Privacy Center is small but it protects trust, so do not leave it for the end.

How the pieces fit

Three flows do most of the work: a trip, a cry for help, and consent. Click any step to see what happens and what the app must do. The diagrams are simplified; the edge cases are in the notes.

Trip flow rules

  • Never show more than one primary action.
  • Keep the help button visible on every step.
  • Cache the route before the trip starts.
  • Let the person advance manually.
  • Boarding is a step. Do not assume seeing the bus means getting on.

Help flow rules

  • Ask “Are you safe?” before “What happened?”.
  • Use a 30-second timeout, not a silent failure.
  • Never auto-dial emergency services.
  • Keep the help card readable at arm’s length.

Consent flow rules

  • Trip-only sharing is the default.
  • Show the caregiver’s view to the person.
  • Pause is a normal action, not an exception.
  • No silent changes by the caregiver.

Five code changes to ship this week

  1. Label the controls. Add accessibilityRole, accessibilityLabel, and accessibilityHint to Button, SavedPlaceCard, TransitOptionCard, and the icon-only back and close buttons. 10 / Test

How to prove it helps

Do not test whether people “like” the app. Test whether they can finish a trip, recover from a problem, and ask for help. Run the test on real routes with real phones.

Who to include

  • 6 to 8 people with intellectual disabilities. Mix reading levels, phone confidence, and support needs. Include at least two people who do not read.
  • 3 caregivers. Include one older caregiver and one support worker who manages several people.
  • 2 transport staff or volunteers. They are the people a help card is shown to.
  • 1 speech and language therapist or AAC (communication aid) specialist. Review the copy and the audio.

How to run it

  • Co-design first. Use paper cards and a phone with a clickable prototype. Ask people to show you how they would use it, not whether they like the colour.
  • Task test second. Two real routes: a direct trip and one with a change. Include a bus that stops but the person does not move, a bus that is not theirs, a bus that does not stop, and a missed stop.
  • Help test third. Role-play a stranger or staff member. Can the person show the card and get the right help without speaking?
  • Caregiver test last. Give them the trip card and three alerts. Measure how fast they understand what to do.

What to measure

Trip completionPercentage of trips finished without a caregiver call. Target: +20% over the current app in the same route test.
BoardingPercentage of bus arrivals where the person boards within 60 seconds without a caregiver prompt. Target: 8 of 10.
Right busPercentage of boardings on the correct route, after checking the number. Target: 9 of 10.
Recovery timeTime from a missed stop or delay to a safe next action. Target: under 2 minutes with the app.
Help successPercentage of help-card uses where a stranger or staff member understands what to do. Target: 8 of 10.
ConfidenceThree-face scale before and after the trip. Target: at least one point higher for first-time users.
Caregiver trustCaregivers can say where the person is and what happens next without opening the map. Target: 9 of 10.
Privacy understandingPeople can explain what is shared and with whom. Target: 8 of 10 can answer in one sentence.
